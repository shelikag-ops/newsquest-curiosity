Keep the curiosity going

Below are two optional collections of questions to explore together. Choose whichever sparks your child’s interest—there is no need to complete them all.

Explore Further — discover something the article didn’t explain

These questions from our session guide invite children to investigate unfamiliar ideas and follow a story further.

  • El Niño: Who named it “the little boy,” and why?
  • Peace prizes: What other peace prizes exist, and what kinds of contributions do they recognise? Why is the Goi Peace Award presented in Japan?
  • Vaccines: What is mRNA? How can a vaccine be personalised for one person? Why does testing and approving a new medicine take so long?
  • Electricity: How does electricity travel from a power station to our homes? Why don’t we put all power lines underground?
  • History: Why was the Treaty of Paris signed in Paris rather than London or Philadelphia? Were there other Treaties of Paris?
  • Accessibility: What makes a wheelchair “all-terrain”? Who decides which places become national parks?
  • Jellyfish: Are jellyfish actually fish? How do they function without a brain, and why do some glow?
  • Social media: Why do many apps set a minimum age of 13? How do features such as autoplay and endless scrolling encourage people to stay longer?

Pick one question, look for a trustworthy source together, and invite your child to explain what they discovered in their own words. What new question does their discovery raise?

Think Beyond the Headlines — consider evidence, choices and different viewpoints

These questions invite discussion rather than a quick factual answer. Encourage your child to explain their reasoning, consider another perspective and be willing to change their mind.

  • Art and community: If you could create one art project to improve our town, what would it be, and why?
  • Recognition: Who would you nominate for a peace award? What evidence would support your choice?
  • Power outages: Should power companies spend more on protecting power lines if everyone’s bills increase? What information would help you decide?
  • Fair access: If a park has only one all-terrain wheelchair, how should it decide who can use it and for how long?
  • Evaluating evidence: If one study says a policy works, what else would you want to know before accepting its conclusion?
  • Climate connections: What evidence would we need before linking a particular flood or storm to climate change? What other factors should we investigate?
  • News judgement: Why do headlines often emphasise a “first”? Does being first always make something important?

Useful follow-up prompts: “What makes you think that?” “Who might see it differently?” “What is missing from the story?” “What evidence could change your mind?”
